Electricity isn't "clean" or "dirty." It depends.

When you charge your phone, the electrons don't come from one labelled source. They come from the grid — the giant shared pool of every power plant running in your region at that exact moment. Some of those plants burn fossil fuels and emit carbon dioxide (CO₂, the main greenhouse gas warming the planet). Others — wind, solar, hydro, nuclear — emit almost none while running.

So the "cleanliness" of your electricity is really a question about the mix of what's generating it right now:

  • Coal — the dirtiest. Roughly 1,000 grams of CO₂ per kWh.
  • Natural gas — about 400 g/kWh. Cleaner than coal, still fossil.
  • Wind, solar, hydro, nuclear — essentially 0 g/kWh while operating.
Carbon intensity is the one number that captures this: the grams of CO₂ emitted per kilowatt-hour of electricity (written gCO₂/kWh). It's a weighted average of whatever's on the grid — burn more coal, the number goes up; add more solar, it goes down.

For context, a gallon of gasoline burned in a car emits about 8,900 grams of CO₂. So a clean kWh is a rounding error; a dirty one adds up.

The twist: it changes by the hour and by the map

By the hour. In California, the sun comes up and solar floods the grid — carbon intensity can fall to ~30 gCO₂/kWh at midday, then climb past 200 after sunset when gas plants take over. Same wall socket, ~8× difference depending on the clock.

By the place. California (hydro, solar, nuclear) is far cleaner than the coal-heavy Midwest. Plug the same heater into the wall in San Francisco vs. rural Missouri and the emissions can differ several-fold.

Why anyone cares: if a kWh can be 8× cleaner at the right moment, then shifting flexible loads — charging your EV, running the dishwasher, scheduling a data center's batch jobs — to clean hours cuts real emissions with zero change in how you live. This is called load shifting or carbon-aware computing, and big companies (Google, Microsoft) do it at scale. Companies like WattTime and Electricity Maps sell exactly this data.

The data puzzle GridClean solves

Here's the catch: no one publishes "carbon intensity right now, for your ZIP code" for free. But two U.S. government datasets each hold half the answer:

EPA eGRID
Emission factors
How much CO₂ each fuel emits per MWh. A static table — no live data.

GridClean
Live gCO₂/kWh
Multiply, sum, divide by total generation — a number neither source publishes.

That's the conceptual heart of the project: a data fusion. Multiply each fuel's live output by its emission factor, add them up, divide by the total electricity — and you've manufactured a valuable real-time metric out of two datasets that couldn't answer the question alone. It's the exact thing commercial services charge for.

The genuinely hard part is geography: the live feed reports by "balancing authority" (the operator of a chunk of grid) while the emissions table is organized by EPA "subregion," and the two maps don't line up neatly. GridClean reconciles them and is honest about the uncertainty — every answer carries a mapping_confidence flag instead of pretending the match is perfect.

What GridClean actually lets you do

Once you can compute that number, useful features fall out of it:

Right now — the live carbon intensity and fuel mix for any ZIP or region.
History & forecast — the last 48 hours, plus a 24-hour prediction (with an honest error margin) of how clean the grid will be.
Cleanest hour — the best upcoming time to run a load, and how much CO₂ you'd save versus the dirtiest hour.
Compare & savings — rank regions cleanest-to-dirtiest; quantify the CO₂ saved by time-shifting a specific load.
Ask the grid — type a plain-English question ("which regions are cleanest right now?") and get an answer, with the database query it ran shown for trust.
Narrated insights — a written summary of a region's recent trend, computed from the data and explained in words.

Glossary

kWh / MWh
Kilowatt-hour and megawatt-hour — units of energy. 1 MWh = 1,000 kWh. A typical home uses ~30 kWh/day; a power plant's output is measured in MWh.
gCO₂/kWh — carbon intensity
Grams of CO₂ emitted per kilowatt-hour of electricity. The headline metric. Lower is cleaner.
Fuel mix
The breakdown of what's generating electricity at a moment — e.g. 40% gas, 25% renewables, 15% nuclear, 20% coal.
Balancing authority
The operator responsible for matching electricity supply and demand across a chunk of the grid (e.g. CISO = California ISO, ERCO = Texas/ERCOT). EIA reports the live mix per balancing authority.
eGRID subregion
EPA's geographic units for reporting emission factors. They don't align 1:1 with balancing authorities — hence GridClean's mapping_confidence.
Average vs. marginal emissions
GridClean computes the average intensity of all generation. "Marginal" asks which plant ramps up for your next kWh — a harder question (and what premium products model). An honest limitation, stated plainly.
Load shifting
Moving flexible electricity use to cleaner hours to cut emissions without using less energy overall.
